Chainring swap

1× chainring size on a 10–51 or a 10–52

On one chainring, the cassette sets the range and the chainring slides the whole ladder. A 30-tooth ring is not a wider gear range than a 32. It is the same range, 6.3% easier at the bottom and 6.3% easier at the top.

The cassettes

These are common published 12-cog sequences. The end teeth are what set the range. The steps in the middle matter when you care about jumps, and they are not identical on these two cassettes.

  • 10–51: 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 21, 24, 28, 33, 39, 45, 51
  • 10–52: 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 21, 24, 28, 32, 36, 42, 52

Range on a 1× is largest cog ÷ smallest cog. A 10–52 is 52 ÷ 10 = 5.20×. A 10–51 is 51 ÷ 10 = 5.10×. Neither number changes when you change the chainring or the wheel. The wheel and the ring change the gear inches. They do not change how many times wider the hard gear is than the easy gear.

Figures below use a 29×2.4 estimate: bead seat 622 mm, labeled width 61 mm, height factor 1, rolling diameter 744 mm. Cadence is 80 rpm. The same ratios on a smaller wheel are lower gear inches. The wheel size guide shows why a 700×28c and this 29×2.4 do not share a chart.

A 32×52 is 18.0 gear inches, 1.44 meters of rollout, and 4.3 mph (6.9 km/h) at 80 rpm. A 30-tooth ring is 30 ÷ 32 of that, which is 6.3% lower: 16.9 gear inches and 4.0 mph in the same cog. The hardest gear falls by the same 6.3%, from 93.7 gear inches (22.3 mph) to 87.9 (20.9 mph). A 34-tooth ring moves both ends the other way, 6.3% higher than the 32. You trade climbing gear for top speed, or the reverse. You do not add a cog.

On a 32-tooth ring, 32×52 is 18.0 gear inches and 32×51 is 18.4. The 52 is 1.9% easier. Both hard gears are 32×10 at 93.7 gear inches, because both cassettes include a 10. Choosing 52 instead of 51 lowers the climb a little and leaves the top end alone. Choosing 30 instead of 32 lowers the climb and the top end together, by 6.3%. Those are different purchases.

At 80 rpm the 34×51 and the 34×52 both round to 4.6 mph. The gear inches still differ: 19.5 versus 19.2. Speed to the nearest tenth of a mile per hour is a coarse ruler for a one-tooth cassette change.

What the tooth span is, and is not

Rear span is 41 teeth on the 10–51 and 42 on the 10–52. With one chainring the front gap is zero, so those are the totals too. The figure is arithmetic for how much chain the rear derailleur must take up. It is not a certificate that a derailleur, a clutch, or a chain will run either cassette. Check the maker’s largest-cog and capacity numbers for the part you have.
