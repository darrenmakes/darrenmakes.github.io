Cassette swap

10–33 versus 10–36, on a 50/34

Both of these are 12-cog cassettes, and both start at 10 teeth. The swap does not buy a harder top gear. It buys a 36-tooth cog in place of a 33, and it leaves most of the ladder where it was.

The two tooth lists

These are common published 12-cog sequences, not a claim that every cassette with those end teeth uses them. Edit the calculator if the cluster in your hand differs.

  • 10–33: 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 17, 19, 21, 24, 28, 33
  • 10–36: 10, 11, 12, 13, 15, 17, 19, 21, 24, 28, 32, 36

Wheel: 700×28c, bead seat 622 mm, tire height equal to the labeled width, rolling diameter 678 mm. Chainrings: 50 and 34. Cadence: 90 rpm.

What changes

Lowest and highest gears. The 10-tooth cog is on both cassettes, so the hardest gear does not move.
EasiestGear inches90 rpmHardest
10–3334×3327.5 in7.4 mph50×10, 133.5 in
10–3634×3625.2 in6.7 mph50×10, 133.5 in

25.2 is 8.3% lower than 27.5. That is the climbing change on the 34-tooth ring: about 2.01 meters per stroke instead of 2.19. The 50×10 is still 10.65 meters and 35.7 mph at 90 rpm. Range goes from 4.85× to 5.29× because the bottom moved and the top did not.

Eight percent is more than the 5.9% from an 11–32 to an 11–34, and less than the 17.6% from an 11–28 to an 11–34. The 10-tooth cog is also a harder top gear than an 11: 50×10 is 133.5 gear inches, against 121.3 for a 50×11 on this same wheel. That difference is already in the 10–33. The 10–36 does not add it.

Twenty-one of 24 combinations are already covered

Each cassette has 12 cogs and two chainrings, so 24 combinations. Twenty-one of the 10–36 combinations sit within 3% of some gear the 10–33 already provides. Three do not:

  • 34×36 is 8.3% easier than the nearest gear you have, 34×33. This is the new climbing gear.
  • 34×32 is 3.1% harder than 34×33. It is just outside the 3% band, and it is a slightly harder step, not an easier climb.
  • 50×32 is 3.1% harder than 50×33. Same kind of step, on the big ring. It does not extend the top.

The two 3.1% rows are barely distinct from cogs you can already use. If you are counting “new gears,” the swap adds one that matters for climbing and two that a rider may not feel as new.

Three more teeth across the cassette

Rear tooth span is the biggest cog minus the smallest. The 10–33 spans 23 teeth. The 10–36 spans 26. Add the 16 teeth between a 50 and a 34 and the totals are 39 and 42. That sum is the usual planning figure for how much chain a rear derailleur must take up. It is arithmetic. It is not a statement that a particular derailleur will shift either cassette, or that it accepts a 10-tooth cog. Read the capacity number and the largest cog the maker publishes for the derailleur you have.
