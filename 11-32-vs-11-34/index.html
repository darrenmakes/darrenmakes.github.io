Cassette swap

11–32 versus 11–34

If the bike already has a 32-tooth cog, an 11–34 does not repeat the big jump you get leaving an 11–28. The new low gear is one cog easier. The 11-tooth top gear is the same cog you already have.

The two tooth lists

These are common published 11-cog sequences, not a claim that every cassette with those end teeth uses them. Edit the calculator if the cluster in your hand differs.

  • 11–32: 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18, 20, 22, 25, 28, 32
  • 11–34: 11, 13, 15, 17, 19, 21, 23, 25, 27, 30, 34

Wheel for every figure below: 700×28c, bead seat 622 mm, tire height equal to the labeled width, rolling diameter 678 mm. Cadence: 90 rpm. The same wheel and the same 11-tooth cog are why the hardest gear depends only on the big chainring.

On a 50/34

Lowest and highest gears on a 50/34. The hardest gear is 50×11 on both cassettes.
EasiestGear inches90 rpmHardest
11–3234×3228.4 in7.6 mph50×11, 121.3 in
11–3434×3426.7 in7.1 mph50×11, 121.3 in

26.7 is 5.9% lower than 28.4. Rollout in that gear goes from 2.26 meters to 2.13. The 50×11 stays 9.68 meters and 32.5 mph. Range goes from 4.28× to 4.55× because the bottom moved and the top did not.

That 5.9% is the whole climbing change on the small ring. It is much smaller than the 11–28 versus 11–34 swap on this same bike, which is 17.6% easier, because an 11–28’s lowest cog is 28, not 32. Going from 32 to 34 teeth is 34 ÷ 32, a short step. Going from 28 to 34 is not.

Fourteen of the 22 gears are already covered

Two chainrings and 11 cogs make 22 combinations. Fourteen of the 11–34 combinations sit within 3% of some gear the 11–32 already provides. Eight do not:

  • 34×34 is 5.9% easier than the nearest gear you have, 34×32. This is the new climbing gear.
  • 34×30 is 6.7% harder than that same 34×32. It is a step up from the old low gear, not an easier one.
  • 34×27 is 3.7% harder than 34×28.
  • 50×34 is 4.8% easier than 34×22. It is the big ring on the new largest cog, still far above the low gear.
  • 34×23 is 4.3% easier than 34×22.
  • 34×21 is 3.6% harder than 50×32.
  • 50×17 is 3.8% harder than 34×12.
  • 50×15 is 6.7% harder than 50×16. It does not raise the top gear. 50×11 is still the hardest.

A 3% band is tighter than a normal one-tooth jump. “Within 3%” means almost the same rollout.

On a 46/30 the low gear moves by the same 5.9%

The percentage is a tooth ratio, 32 versus 34, so the chainring sizes do not change it. The gear inches do change, because a 30-tooth ring is smaller than a 34.

The same cassettes on a 46/30. The hardest gear is 46×11 on both.
EasiestGear inches90 rpmHardest
11–3230×3225.0 in6.7 mph46×11, 111.6 in
11–3430×3423.6 in6.3 mph46×11, 111.6 in

23.6 is 5.9% lower than 25.0. Rollout goes from 2.00 meters to 1.88. The 46×11 stays 8.91 meters and 29.9 mph. Range goes from 4.46× to 4.74×. Twelve of the 22 combinations on the 11–34 sit within 3% of a gear the 11–32 already has. The other ten are steps in the middle of the range. The only one that extends the bottom is 30×34, and nothing extends the top past 46×11.

A 46/30 with an 11–32 is already easier, at 25.0 gear inches, than a 50/34 with an 11–34, at 26.7. If the goal is a lower gear, the chainring you start from matters as much as the two extra teeth on the cassette. The chart does not say which of those you should change.

The derailleur has to swallow two more teeth

Rear tooth span is the biggest cog minus the smallest. The 11–32 spans 21 teeth. The 11–34 spans 23. A 50/34 and a 46/30 both leave 16 teeth between the chainrings, so the totals are 37 and 39. That sum is the usual planning figure for how much chain a rear derailleur must take up. It is arithmetic. It is not a statement that a particular derailleur will shift either cassette. Read the capacity number and the largest cog the maker publishes for the derailleur you have.
