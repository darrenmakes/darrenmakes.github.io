Formula

Gear inches, worked by hand

A gear inch is the diameter of a direct-drive wheel that would travel as far, per pedal stroke, as the gear you are in. Bigger is harder and faster. Smaller is easier.

The formula

Count teeth, then measure the wheel that actually rolls, not the number printed on an old catalog page.

gear inches = (chainring ÷ cog) × rolling diameter in inches

The rolling diameter used here is the rim’s ISO bead-seat diameter plus twice the tire’s height. The default treats height as the labeled width. A 700c rim is 622 mm at the bead seat. A 28 mm tire at a height factor of 1 makes the rolling diameter 622 + 56 = 678 mm, which is 26.69 inches.

50×11 and 34×28 on that wheel

Ratio first. 50 ÷ 11 = 4.55. 34 ÷ 28 = 1.21. Multiply each by 26.69 inches.

The range of that drivetrain is 121.3 ÷ 32.4, which is the same as dividing the ratios: 3.74×. The hard gear covers about 3.74 times as much ground per stroke as the easy gear.

A one-tooth step at the small end of a cassette is a real change, not a duplicate. 50×11 versus 50×12 is about 9%. Cogspan marks a gear as close only when another combination on the same bike is within 3%, which is tighter than a normal one-tooth jump. On a double, that usually means the same rollout reached with the other ring, such as 50×16 and 34×11.

What changes the number

  • More teeth on the chainring, or fewer on the cog, raises gear inches.
  • A larger wheel or a taller tire raises every gear, even when the tooth counts stay put. The wheel size guide puts the same 50×11 on several rims.
  • A bigger largest cog lowers the easy gear. An 11–28 versus 11–34 moves it more than an 11–32 versus 11–34, and a 10–33 versus 10–36 sits between them. A smaller 1× chainring lowers every gear by the same fraction and does not widen the range.
  • Gear inches do not include crank length. Gain ratio does: wheel radius divided by crank length, times the gear ratio. The calculator shows it when you enter a crank length.

These are planning figures. A worn tire, a liner, or a heavy load changes the rolling diameter a little. If you are comparing two wheels that should be close, measure one revolution and use that instead of the estimate.
